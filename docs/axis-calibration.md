# Calibrating the axes

> **Draft for review by a practising special-education teacher.** The levels
> below are anchored in observable classroom behaviour rather than adjectives.
> If a descriptor does not match what you see in a real child, it is wrong and
> should be changed.

## Why this matters more than it looks

Recipes trigger on levels: `COG>=2`, `DEC>=2`. If `COG:2` means something
different in every school — or something different next term in the same school —
then recipes are not portable, a shared corpus is built on sand, and a handover
packet is noise with a nice format.

"Moderate" is not calibratable. *"Loses the thread when more than three things
are on the page"* is.

## Four rules before you score anything

**1. Score what you have seen, not what the report says.** A diagnosis is not an
axis. If you have not observed it in your classroom, leave it `null`.

**2. Score the barrier, not the residual.** Score what happens **without** the
supports currently in place. A support that works hides the barrier, and the next
teacher — or the next unit — will underestimate it. If she copes fine *because*
you always give her one task per page, that is `COG` high, not `COG` low.

**3. Between two levels, take the lower one and say so.** Under-adapting is
visible: the child struggles, you notice, you adjust upward. Over-adapting is
invisible: the worksheet looks kind and the curriculum has quietly gone. This
project's whole failure mode is the second one.

**4. If a subject differs, note it.** A child can be `LIN:1` in Maths and `LIN:3`
in Literature. Score the general case and record the exception.

## The levels

`0` no barrier · `1` mild · `2` moderate · `3` severe or total · `null` not observed

Each axis below gives the behaviour you would see, and — more usefully — **how to
tell the levels apart when it is not obvious**.

---

### `PER-V` · Visual access

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Uses standard print with no visual adjustment |
| 1 | Uses standard print but tires, loses their place, or leans in close |
| 2 | Cannot use standard print. Works with enlarged type, high contrast or magnification — **vision is still the route** |
| 3 | Vision is not a usable route for print. Braille, audio or tactile material |

**Telling 2 from 3:** at 2 you change the *presentation*. At 3 you change the
*modality*. If enlarging it would help at all, it is 2.

---

### `PER-A` · Auditory access

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Follows spoken instruction in an ordinary noisy classroom |
| 1 | Follows with front seating, repetition or visual support; loses content when the room is loud |
| 2 | Cannot rely on speech alone. Needs instructions in writing as well |
| 3 | Audio carries nothing usable. Sign language and/or written material |

**Telling 2 from 3:** at 2, speech helps when supported. At 3, speech is not a
channel — supporting it does not make it one.

---

### `DEC` · Decoding

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Reads age-typical text accurately and fluently |
| 1 | Reads accurately but slowly. The effort shows on longer texts |
| 2 | Decoding eats so much effort that comprehension suffers, even on text they would understand if someone read it |
| 3 | Cannot access text by reading it. Needs it read aloud or as audio |

**The test that separates `DEC` from `LIN`:** read it aloud to them.

- If reading it aloud changes what they can do → the barrier is **DEC**.
- If reading it aloud changes nothing → the barrier is **LIN**.

This single check is the most common scoring error in the whole schema, and it
matters because the two axes pull toward opposite adaptations: `DEC` wants audio
and typography, `LIN` wants simplified language. Get it backwards and you simplify
the curriculum out of a child whose only problem was the font.

---

### `LIN` · Linguistic comprehension

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Understands age-typical language |
| 1 | Understands, but misses low-frequency vocabulary, idiom or figurative language |
| 2 | Loses meaning in sentences with subordinate clauses or more than one idea. Needs vocabulary pre-taught |
| 3 | Needs short, literal statements. Complex language carries no meaning at all |

**Telling 1 from 2:** at 1 the problem is *words*. At 2 it is *structure* — they
know every word in the sentence and still cannot say what it asks.

---

### `COG` · Cognitive load and working memory

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Works an ordinary page |
| 1 | Copes with the page but loses the thread in multi-step tasks |
| 2 | Loses the thread when more than about three things are present or required at once |
| 3 | Holds one or two at a time. A second instruction displaces the first |

**Telling `COG` from `ATE`:** `COG` is **how much at once**. `ATE` is **how
long**. A child who works well for twenty minutes on a page with one exercise, but
falls apart on a page with six, is `COG` — not inattentive.

---

### `ATE` · Attention

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Sustains a task to completion |
| 1 | Sustains, but is pulled away by nearby noise or movement |
| 2 | A few minutes before needing redirection. Distractors **on the page itself** pull them off |
| 3 | Works in bursts. The task must be short enough to finish inside one burst |

**Useful measure:** roughly how long between starting and the first time you have
to bring them back. Note the number; it calibrates better than the label.

---

### `EJE` · Executive function

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Plans, starts and checks their own work |
| 1 | Starts and organises given a prompt |
| 2 | Needs the steps supplied. Does not spontaneously sequence a task |
| 3 | Does not start without the first step done for them or an adult alongside. Cannot tell how far along they are |

**Where to look:** the first sixty seconds after the sheet lands on the desk.
What happens in that minute tells you more than the rest of the lesson.

---

### `MOT` · Motor and response

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Writes at age-typical speed and legibility |
| 1 | Writes, but slowly or with fatigue. Quantity limits what they can show you |
| 2 | Handwriting is not viable for extended answers. Needs typing, a scribe or oral response |
| 3 | Needs an alternative access method for any response at all |

**Note:** this axis is about **showing what they know**, not about knowing it.
Scoring it high never justifies simplifying content.

---

### `REG` · Sensory regulation

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Unaffected by ordinary classroom stimulation |
| 1 | Copes. Prefers calm and predictability |
| 2 | Specific stimuli or unpredictability visibly degrade the work |
| 3 | Specific stimuli or unpredictability end the session |

**Ground it in fact:** what has actually ended a session? Write that in `avoid`.
It is worth more than the number.

---

### `CUR` · Curricular gap

| | What you see |
|---|---|
| 0 | Working at year-group level |
| 1 | Below year group, but inside this year's content with support |
| 2 | Working on a previous year's content — roughly a two-year gap |
| 3 | Working on content substantially removed from the year group |

**Read this one differently.** `CUR` never drives a recipe on its own. It is a
**flag**: at 2 or above you are in significant-adaptation territory, where
objectives and assessment criteria change. That is a decision for the teaching
team and the learner's official file — not for a tool, and not for one teacher
alone.

---

## Doing this for a real child in ten minutes

1. Take one recent worksheet they struggled with.
2. Ask, in order: could they **see** it, **hear** the instruction, **read** it,
   **understand** it once read, hold **how much** it asked, **for how long**,
   **start** it, and **answer** it?
3. Each question maps to one axis, in order: `PER-V`, `PER-A`, `DEC`, `LIN`,
   `COG`, `ATE`, `EJE`, `MOT`.
4. `REG` comes from what you avoid without thinking about it any more.
5. `CUR` comes from the official file, not from you.
6. Anything you did not actually observe stays `null`. It will get filled in.

## What we want reviewed

- Does any descriptor not match a real child you have taught?
- Is the `DEC` / `LIN` distinction the right one, and is the read-aloud test how
  you would actually make it?
- Is the `COG` / `ATE` split ("how much" vs "how long") real in practice?
- Is "score the barrier, not the residual" right? It is the rule we are least
  sure of, and it is the one that decides whether handover works.
- Is anything missing that you use every day and is not an axis here?
